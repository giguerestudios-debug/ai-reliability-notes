# Why You Should Never Trust an AI Just Because It Agrees With You

*By Antoine Giguère*

The most dangerous thing an AI ever did to me was agree with me. It didn't crash or lie in an obvious way. It just went along. I run a small studio in Querétaro, and every week I use AI for code, project plans and client work. I like these tools a lot. But a tool that wants to please you isn't the same as a tool that tells you the truth, and I learned that the hard way. If you use AI for decisions that matter, check it. Every time.

Here's what it looks like. I'm deep in a long chat, working through a website build or a decision about a client. I suggest an approach and the AI goes with it. I push back and it flips, politely, as if it had been on my side all along. Fine. But a dozen questions later the answers start to drift. Details get invented. The goal quietly changes. Nothing sounds wrong, and that's the problem. A good colleague argues with you. An AI that always folds is an echo with a nice voice.

Numbers are worse, because they come wrapped in confidence. Someone I know told me they used ChatGPT to work out taxes for a large company and ended up about $160,000 off. When they asked whether everything was correct, it said yes. They told me the mistake cost them their job. I can't verify that story, and I'm not naming anyone, but I believe it, because I've watched AI hand me wrong totals and wrong client details in the same calm tone it uses when it's right. Asking it "is this correct?" is asking the author to grade their own homework.

There's a third problem, and it's about us, not accuracy. These systems can be talked into things. With enough clever prompting, people get chatbots to help with hacking or to suggest dangerous things they should refuse. I won't describe how, and that's not the point. If a bot can be coaxed into saying something harmful, it can be coaxed into saying something false, and a person who trusts it won't see the difference. This isn't one company's issue. I've caught Claude making things up too.

I can't deny the upside. AI has saved me hours, and I'm not giving it up. So use it. Just don't hand it the keys. Re-add the totals yourself. Ask it to argue against you. Verify anything that could cost someone money or a job. And if you build these tools, treat this as a safety problem, not a footnote. Trust, but check the math.
